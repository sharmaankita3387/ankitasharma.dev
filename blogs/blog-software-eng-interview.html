What Actually Happens in a Software Engineering Interview?

Aug 15, 2026 • 5 min read • By Ankita Sharma

When you first start applying for software engineering roles, the interview process often feels like a mysterious black box. You hear buzzwords like online assessments, system design rounds, and behavioral screenings, but it is hard to visualize what actually takes place from the moment you hit submit to the moment a decision lands in your inbox.

Here is the standard four stage roadmap most tech companies follow, along with exactly what interviewers are evaluating at each step.

"Software engineering interviews are not just about raw coding speed. You need DSA for the OAs, communication skills for behavioural rounds, and solid, deeply understood projects for technical deep dives."

1. Online Assessment (OA)

This is almost always your first gatekeeper after resume screening. You will receive an automated link (typically via HackerRank, CodeSignal, or Karat) requiring you to solve timed programming problems without a live human interviewer present.

Common core topics tested include:

  • Arrays & Strings
  • Hash Maps & Sets
  • Linked Lists
  • Trees & Binary Search Trees
  • Graphs (BFS & DFS)
  • Dynamic Programming (common at larger tech firms)

This is why early practice with Data Structures & Algorithms (DSA) is essential. A massive percentage of candidates get filtered out at this stage simply because they have not practiced solving problems under strict time pressure.

2. Behavioural Interview

This round focuses on who you are as a team member, communicator, and problem solver. Software engineering is a collaborative discipline; writing brilliant code does not matter if nobody can work with you.

Expect questions along these lines:

  • "Tell me about yourself and what drawn you to software engineering."
  • "Why do you want to work at this company specifically?"
  • "Tell me about a technical challenge you faced and how you overcame it."
  • "Describe a time you collaborated in a cross functional team."
  • "Tell me about a project that failed and what you took away from the experience."

Companies are looking for clear answers to three questions: Can you articulate your thought process? How do you handle feedback and friction? Are you someone the team would genuinely enjoy working alongside every day?

3. Technical Interview (Live Round)

This is a live session, usually 45 to 60 minutes, with an engineer on the team. For interns, new grads, and early career candidates, interviewers spend considerable time probing your personal projects and resume line items.

Be prepared to discuss:

  • Motivation: Why you decided to build each project and what problem it solves.
  • Architecture & Tech Stack: Why you selected specific languages, frameworks, or databases over alternatives.
  • Trade offs & Roadblocks: Real challenges you hit during development and how you debugged them.
  • Next Steps: What architectural or feature improvements you would make if you had another month to build.

You will also typically solve one or two coding problems in real time on an interactive editor. Most fall in the LeetCode Easy to Medium spectrum, although some companies lean toward Medium to Hard problems. The key here is thinking out loud: explain your edge cases, analyze your Time and Space complexity ($O(N)$ vs $O(N^2)$), and talk through your approach before typing a single character.

4. Offer or Rejection

Once you reach this stage, you have done everything within your control.

If a rejection email arrives, do not panic or take it as a reflection of your potential. Every single interview provides actionable diagnostic data:

  • It highlights the specific algorithmic patterns you need to revisit.
  • It reveals what questions engineering teams are actually prioritizing right now.
  • It sharpens your live communication for the next opportunity.

Treat the recruiting journey as an iterative loop: sharpen your DSA, refine your behavioral stories, and build projects you can speak about with deep ownership.
