Best Free DSA Resources for Beginners

Oct 17, 2026 • 6 min read • By Ankita Sharma

One of the biggest misconceptions about learning Data Structures and Algorithms is that you need to purchase expensive courses or bootcamps to succeed.

You do not.

Everything you need to build a rock solid algorithmic foundation is completely available for free online. Here are the premier resources I recommend to every engineering student starting their DSA journey.

"The resource itself is not what makes you successful. Your day to day consistency does. One focused hour a day compounds further than most people realize."

1. VisuAlgo

Direct Link: visualgo.net
Best for: Visualizing abstract concepts step by step.

If you find it difficult to mentally track how pointers move or trees rebalance, start here. VisuAlgo provides interactive animations across arrays, linked lists, binary search trees, graph traversals, and classic sorting algorithms. Rather than memorizing dry pseudocode, you can watch data structures update in real time.

2. FreeCodeCamp 50 Hour DSA Course

Direct Link: FreeCodeCamp on YouTube
Best for: Comprehensive, structured instruction from zero.

This full length lecture series guides students from absolute fundamentals through to advanced techniques. It covers Big O notation, asymptotic bounds, data structures, and practical problem solving strategies in a methodical sequence.

3. NeetCode

Direct Link: neetcode.io
Best for: Targeted technical interview preparation.

One of the biggest obstacles beginners face on LeetCode is knowing which problems to solve out of thousands. NeetCode solves that dilemma by providing organized roadmaps, categorized lists, and video walkthroughs that explain the underlying mental models cleanly.

4. LeetCode

Direct Link: leetcode.com
Best for: Practical coding execution and test runner validation.

This is where theory turns into practice. Almost all industry Online Assessments and live technical interviews draw from patterns present on LeetCode. Start with Easy tier problems, focus on edge cases, and avoid jumping straight into Hard problems until your fundamentals are rock solid.

5. Harvard CS50

Direct Link: cs50.harvard.edu
Best for: Low level computer science principles and memory awareness.

While CS50 is an introductory computer science course rather than a pure DSA drill, it instills the rigorous computational thinking needed for algorithmic success. You will explore memory allocation, pointers, pointers arithmetic, arrays, and algorithms from first principles.

6. GeeksforGeeks

Direct Link: geeksforgeeks.org
Best for: Instant reference and code syntax examples.

Whenever you hit a roadblock on a specific method or theorem, GeeksforGeeks provides clear articles, code implementations across multiple languages, and concise breakdowns. Think of it as a comprehensive computer science encyclopedia.

7. Dedicated YouTube Instructors

Best for: Alternative explanations when a concept does not click immediately.

Sometimes one creator explains recursion or dynamic programming in a way that suddenly makes everything click. Exceptional channels include Abdul Bari, William Fiset, CS Dojo, and NeetCode. Never hesitate to look at an alternative perspective if an explanation feels unclear.

A Three Month Action Blueprint

If I were starting from scratch today, here is how I would structure my time:

  • Month One: Master data structure mechanics visually on VisuAlgo while completing foundational lectures on FreeCodeCamp.
  • Month Two: Begin solving Easy tier problems on LeetCode, using GeeksforGeeks for syntax lookups and concept refreshes.
  • Month Three and Beyond: Follow the curated NeetCode roadmap, practice under real time constraints, and review weak patterns every weekend.

A Crucial Warning: Avoid Resource Hoarding

A widespread trap among beginners is spending hours bookmarking courses, downloading guides, and collecting playlists instead of actually writing code.

You do not need dozens of resources open simultaneously. Choose two or three trusted platforms and commit to daily problem solving. Progress comes from active coding, not passive collecting.
