How I'd Spend My First Year of Engineering If I Could Do It Again

Sep 12, 2026 • 6 min read • By Ankita Sharma

One of the most common questions students ask during their first semester is what they should be doing right now to land an internship after first year.

The good news is that you do not need to do everything at once. In fact, trying to do everything simultaneously is usually the quickest path to burnout.

If I could start my engineering journey all over again, here is the exact roadmap I would follow.

"You do not need to know everything on day one. You simply need consistency. A little deliberate progress every single day compounds far more than most students realize."

1. Join One Software Related Club

Not three clubs. Not four clubs. Just one.

Many students assume that joining multiple student societies will make their resume look impressive. In reality, spreading yourself too thin means you rarely contribute meaningfully to any of them.

Instead, pick one software related club and commit to it fully. Great options include:

  • Google Developer Groups on campus
  • AWS Cloud Clubs
  • Microsoft Learn Student Ambassadors
  • Computer Science and Software societies
  • Competitive hackathon design teams

Even a modest role provides practical teamwork skills, networking opportunities, and strong bullet points for your resume. Most importantly, it introduces you to peers who share your drive.

2. Learn DSA Consistently

If your goal is early internships, Data Structures and Algorithms must become a daily habit.

I would set aside about one hour each day working through a structured roadmap. The FreeCodeCamp 50 hour DSA course on YouTube is a wonderful resource because it guides students step by step from baseline principles to advanced patterns.

The key is never speed; the key is consistency. One hour every day for several months will prepare you far better than cramming for twelve hours a day during recruiting season. Most students know DSA matters, but very few stay consistent long enough to feel truly comfortable with it.

3. Learn a Skill, Then Immediately Build With It

This is the exact trap where many students get stuck: spending months watching tutorials without writing any independent code.

My strategy would follow a simple two phase loop:

  • Weeks One and Two: Spend one hour per day learning the syntax and mental models of a new tool, such as HTML and CSS, modern JavaScript, Python, SQL, or Git and GitHub.
  • Weeks Three and Four: Spend one hour per day building a standalone project using that skill.

Your early projects do not need to be revolutionary. They just need to prove that you understand core principles. The goal is discovering the gaps in your knowledge, because building projects exposes the exact weaknesses that guided tutorials hide.

4. Focus on Deep Understanding Rather Than Speed

One observation I have made about engineering is that the strongest practitioners are rarely the ones who race through topics the fastest. They are the people who understand systems at a foundational level.

They take the time to understand:

  • Why a specific algorithm works
  • Why a particular architectural choice was selected
  • Why one data structure is superior to another for a given problem
  • The performance and security trade offs of the code they deploy

Deep understanding produces exceptional engineers, and exceptional engineers will always be in high demand.

5. Build at Least Three Independent Projects

By the conclusion of your first year, aim to have completed at least three original projects.

This is not about checking off an arbitrary quota for recruiters. It is about having real engineering stories to discuss when interview season arrives. Original projects give interviewers tangible proof that you can apply classroom theory to practical problems. They also help you discover which subfields of software engineering you genuinely enjoy.

6. Prepare for Behavioural Interviews Early

Many students focus exclusively on coding problems and completely overlook behavioural preparation.

Engineering teams do not just hire people who can write code; they hire people they want to collaborate with every single day. Start reflecting on your experiences and organizing stories around teamwork, leadership, technical setbacks, and problem solving.

Your examples do not require prior corporate experience. You can pull high quality stories from coursework, student clubs, hackathons, volunteer initiatives, and part time customer service roles.

7. Remember What Makes an Engineer Truly Valuable

AI tools have become remarkably capable at generating syntax. Today, almost anyone can prompt an assistant to write a code snippet.

However, there is an immense difference between generating code and understanding code. A software engineer knows:

  • Whether the generated logic is actually correct and handles edge cases
  • Whether the system is secure and performant
  • Whether the codebase is maintainable and scalable over time
  • Whether the architecture solves the actual business requirement

The future is not about competing with AI; it is about being the engineer who understands what high quality software looks like. Companies will always need engineers who can review code, improve systems, maintain reliability, and take full architectural responsibility.

Summary Checklist

If I could restart my first year tomorrow, my plan would remain clear:

  • Join one software related student club
  • Practice Data Structures and Algorithms for one hour every day
  • Learn a skill and immediately build an original project with it
  • Complete at least three projects by the end of the year
  • Prepare for behavioural interview questions
  • Prioritize foundational understanding over superficial speed
"No gatekeeping. No ego. Just builders helping builders."
