Interview Prep

Technical Interview Tips for Software Engineering Students

Aug 29, 2026 • 6 min read • By Ankita Sharma

If you have passed the Online Assessment, congratulations. You have reached the stage that many students fear the most: the live technical interview.

The good news is that technical interviews are not designed to trick or embarrass you. Most interviewers simply want to understand how you think, how you unpack ambiguous problems, and whether you genuinely understand the technologies and projects listed on your resume.

"Your resume gets you the interview, but your ability to explain your work gets you the offer. Approach the session like a technical whiteboard conversation with a teammate, not a pop quiz."

1. Master the Fundamentals

Most technical interviews do not begin with the most difficult coding challenge imaginable. Instead, engineers often start with conversational fundamentals to gauge the strength of your foundation.

Common baseline questions include:

  • What is the difference between a class and an object?
  • How do inheritance and polymorphism differ in practice?
  • What are the operational trade offs between a stack and a queue?
  • What makes an API RESTful?
  • When would you choose a relational SQL database over a NoSQL document store?

You do not need to memorize textbook definitions word for word. Focus on understanding the core mental models well enough to explain them in plain English. If you cannot explain a concept simply, you have not internalized it deeply enough yet.

2. Know Your Resume Inside Out

A frequent pitfall for students is listing every buzzword or library they touched once in a quick tutorial. If a framework, language, or tool is written on your resume, treat it as entirely open for discussion.

Interviewers love to dig into specifics:

  • "Why did you choose Python over Node.js for this backend?"
  • "How did you handle state management across components?"
  • "What bottleneck did this database index solve?"

A concise, tightly focused resume filled with technologies you can defend under scrutiny will always outperform a bloated resume padded with buzzwords you cannot explain.

3. Build Projects That Match the Role

One of the highest leverage ways to stand out as an applicant is deliberately aligning your personal projects with the specific domain of the role:

  • Cloud Engineering: Serverless architectures, automated build pipelines, Terraform infrastructure as code, and DNS content delivery deployments.
  • Frontend Engineering: Accessible component systems, responsive layouts, web performance optimizations, and stateful web applications.
  • Backend Engineering: Robust REST or GraphQL APIs, relational database schemas, authentication middleware, and background worker queues.

Walking into an interview and saying that you noticed their team works with serverless pipelines, so you built an architecture using cloud storage and serverless functions to gain direct hands on experience, demonstrates immense initiative and real engineering curiosity.

4. Know Your Projects as a Story

For internship candidates, interviewers routinely spend substantial time dissecting project architecture. Be prepared to break down your builds with clarity:

  • The Problem: Why did this project need to exist? What pain point does it solve?
  • Architecture Decisions: Why did you choose your specific tech stack?
  • The Roadblocks: What broke during development, and how did you debug it?
  • Trade Offs: What compromises did you make to ship it on time?
  • Retrospective: What architectural choices would you refactor if you rebuilt it today?

Interviewers care far more about your decision making trail than whether your project has a flashy landing page.

5. Expect Coding Questions and Think Out Loud

Most live technical rounds incorporate one or two algorithmic coding problems, typically easy to medium difficulty.

The biggest misconception is assuming the interviewer only cares about passing the test suite. A candidate who silently types out a working answer is often rated lower than a candidate who collaborates, asks clarifying questions, talks through edge cases, and discusses runtime and memory complexity out loud.

6. Treat Communication as a Technical Skill

Technical interviews evaluate teamwork as much as technical prowess. Engineering is a collaborative sport; companies need to know whether working with you five days a week will be productive and energizing.

Keep your tone calm, conversational, and professional. Think of your interviewer as a senior peer collaborating on an architectural spike with you.

7. It Is Okay Not to Know Everything

Hitting a wall on a question does not mean the interview is lost. Interviewers know students are still developing their expertise.

When you encounter an unfamiliar concept:

  • Take a steady breath and do not panic.
  • Be candid: acknowledge that you have not worked with that specific tool yet.
  • Share what you do know that is adjacent, and reason through how you would explore or diagnose the problem.

Demonstrating how you learn and reason through ambiguity is often just as persuasive as already having the answer memorized.

"No gatekeeping. No ego. Just builders helping builders."
